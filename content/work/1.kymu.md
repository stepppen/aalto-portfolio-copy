context: |
  Children with neuromuscular conditions need physiotherapy for years, and much of it has to happen at home. That's hard to keep up. Exercises get repetitive, families carry a lot of logistics, and therapists see little of what happens between appointments.

  Kymu turns the home exercises into games and sends objective data like range of motion and balance back to the therapist, who can adjust the plan remotely. It started as my bachelor thesis with Elia Salerno at ZHdK. Since then it has become a funded research project, and I now spend 20% of my week on it.

::cs-section{n="01" title="Research"}
We wanted something that matters to patients and also makes sense for the healthcare system. So we started from the evidence on paediatric neuromuscular therapy. Literature reviews and expert interviews kept pointing at the same problems: low adherence to home exercises, little data to personalise therapy, and a heavy logistical load on families.

::cs-section{n="02" title="Therapist platform"}
Therapists set up each child's programme, then see how the sessions went: movement data over time, pain markers, and a summary they can act on. The goal was to give them a clear picture of the days they never see.

::cs-section{n="03" title="App and TV"}
At home, the phone is the remote and the TV is the stage. The child plays and a camera tracks the movement. The companion app keeps caregivers in the loop without turning them into therapists.

::cs-section{n="05" title="Testing with patients and therapists"}
We worked in two-week sprints, each with clear design and development goals. Every sprint ended with expert interviews and user tests, which decided what changed in the next one. Today, usability tests run with patients and physiotherapists from Lucerne Cantonal Hospital.
